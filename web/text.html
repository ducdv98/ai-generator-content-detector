Text Watermark Lab

How language models hide a signature in their word choices, and what the evidence looks like when you have the key.

What this page can and cannot show. It runs the two published watermark algorithms with a key you can see, so you can make a marked text and watch a detector find it. It cannot tell you whether a text came from Gemini, Claude or ChatGPT: they use the same idea with private keys, and none of them offers a public text detector.

How the marks work

Green-list (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023). Before each word, the key and the previous token split the vocabulary into a green quarter and a red three quarters. Green tokens get a small bonus, so the model picks them more often. The detector redoes the split for every token and counts greens: unmarked text hits green 25% of the time, marked text far more.

Tournament (SynthID-Text, Google DeepMind, 2024; the family Gemini and Claude use). The key and the previous four tokens give every candidate 30 random bits. Candidates drawn from the model play a knockout tournament where the higher bit wins each round. Probabilities stay the same on average, but the winners carry more 1-bits than chance. The detector averages those bits: 0.5 means no mark.

Hidden characters. Zero-width and look-alike space characters. This is the only check that needs no key, and the easiest mark to remove. The three large vendors say their watermarks do not use it.

Both statistical checks need the same key and the same tokenizer that wrote the text. With a different key the pattern is indistinguishable from chance: change "Key used to read" to see it. More detail and sources: docs/text-watermarking.md.
